Scope

What I am accountable for.

Jennmar is a $1B+ global manufacturer of ground-control products for mining and tunneling, privately held and private-equity-backed. Reedsville makes roof bolts, cable bolts, plates, mats, and discs for underground mines in soft and hard rock. About 80 SKUs across 11 processes on 27 production assets, two shifts, five days. Demand follows mining cycles and roof conditions, so winters run heavier.

The P&L

Reedsville is a profit center with its own revenue recognition. Revenue, materials, labor, freight, operating expense, and SG&A sit with the plant. Capital is proposed annually and approved above the plant; the 2026 plan was funded.

Customers and demand

Sales owns the customer relationship. The plant is measured on delivering what sales brings in, so S&OP is mine. I join the VP of Operations and the sales team for onboarding visits with new mining customers. Out of that came the plant's first sales budget, seasonality in the forecast, and slow-moving stock worked down with sales.

Supply and spend

Purchasing issues the orders and sets terms. The plant decides what to order, when, and which critical spares to hold. On project work I negotiate scope and price, with two or more bids on larger purchases.

People

Seven direct reports: Superintendent (72 people), Maintenance Manager (12), Quality Manager (3), Dispatcher (3), Safety Manager, HR and Payroll Specialist, Inventory Control. Ninety hourly, ten salaried. Hiring authority for every role. New operators join through the company's services division for 90 days before going full-time.

Safety and environment

Safety runs on leading indicators: layered audits, near-miss reporting, guarding that removes the hazard, dust collection. No citations. I am accountable for the plant's stormwater and waste permits, with primary and secondary containment added and no notices of violation.

Six plants

Built at one plant, in use at six.

Across all six plants, the division executive team follows sales to budget and open safety actions through the Daily Operations Review. The Reedsville team built it.

Most of what we built has been handed to IT, which owns it and is scaling it. That is the arrangement I wanted. Build the first version with the plant, prove it, hand it over, help the next plant stand it up.

A decision across plants

When Reedsville was running well over its sales budget and a sister plant had capacity to spare, I proposed moving volume there. The VP of Operations and I took the decision jointly. Both plants met budget. Reedsville's overtime fell from 92 to 82 hours per employee biweekly over four months and held after the volume came back. A separate equipment move to a sister plant saves $35K a year in freight.

Daily Operations Review

All six plants in the division

An automated daily email and dashboard: safety, machine availability, inventory, sales to budget, quality, headcount, capex, lean. Built with the plant team, demonstrated to the executive team, then stood up at each plant in person.

Financial Forecaster

All six plants in the division

Projects month-to-date net income every day from ERP spend and delivery data plus a labor estimate. Its charts land in the Daily Operations Review automatically. Plants see cost against budget during the month, not after it. The projection is within about 10% of the month-end actual today; daily labor data should bring that to 3 to 5%.

Quality governance

All six plants in the division

Automated tensile test logs, SPC by heat number, Pareto, and tagged quarantine, rolled out plant by plant. The same logs settled a product complaint. With engineering, we traced it to the customer's installation equipment over-torquing the product. Once corrected, the issue disappeared and the relationship strengthened.

People and decisions

How I lead, and three things that happened.

The pattern has held at every plant. Walk the floor, find the constraint, build the system, develop the people, repeat. The lines below are from my article in Metals & Mining Review; the stories are from Reedsville.

  1. 01

    When people understand what matters most, why it matters and how their work contributes, they are much more likely to take ownership of the result.

    A target on a board is not a priority until the team knows what it means for the work in front of them.

  2. 02

    Data should make problems easier to see, not create more work to find them.

    I automate the flow of information so losses are visible without anyone assembling a report. Pareto and SPC point at the constraint; operators supply the context the data cannot.

  3. 03

    A problem that can quietly return was never really solved.

    The work is finished when the operation behaves differently because of what was learned, and the fix is built into the process.

  4. 04

    The fastest way to stop seeing problems is to make people afraid to bring them forward.

    Accountability starts with what happened and what needs to change, and it runs in both directions, so effort and problem-solving are recognized when things go well.

  5. 05

    The real test is whether the improvement continues when the spotlight moved somewhere else.

    The maintenance area at Messer still ran the same way months after I stopped visiting. The Reedsville systems were handed to IT on purpose, so they do not depend on me.

Succession

When I arrived, the plant ordered a fixed amount of material every month regardless of demand. The superintendent, nearly forty years with the company, had a reason: running short had always cost more than carrying extra. We split the SKUs into make-to-stock and make-to-order. We started with the low-risk make-to-order items, then set min/max levels tied to demand and on-hand stock for the rest. He came along with it.

When he retired, the production supervisor I had been developing stepped into the role as planned. Six months later inventory was down 27% and the storage areas were labeled, inside capacity, and safer.

Culture

I kept the family culture the plant already had. What changed was the reflex, from reacting to problems to preventing them. Most of the leadership team made that shift. A few did not, after ample time and conversation, and I changed those seats.

One I got wrong

During a two-month slowdown, order intake dropped about 20%, and I let headcount drift down without replacing the people who left. Orders came back to normal over three days. Within 48 hours we moved shifts from eight to nine hours and ran a few mandatory Saturdays. I told the VP of Operations on day two. We met every delivery commitment, and the cost was a sizable overtime bill. The swing was seasonal and sharper than usual, since roof conditions in the mines drive orders up in cold months. Seasonality is now built into the demand forecast, staffing plan, and inventory levels.
